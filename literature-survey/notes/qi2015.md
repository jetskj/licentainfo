# Qi et al. (2015) — An Analysis of Patch Plausibility and Correctness for Generate-and-Validate Patch Generation Systems

**Key:** `qi2015` · **Type:** conference paper, ISSTA 2015, pp. 24–36 · **DOI:** [10.1145/2771783.2771791](https://doi.org/10.1145/2771783.2771791) · **PDF:** `papers/qi2015_plausibility.pdf` (MIT-CSAIL-TR-2015-020 author version)

**Cluster:** Patch correctness · **Used in:** Related work; rationale for O2 and for the `degenerat` verdict

## What it establishes

The paper re-analyses the reported patches of three generate-and-validate systems (GenProg, RSRepair, AE). Its findings, stated in the abstract:

1. **Because of errors in the patch evaluation infrastructure, the majority of the reported patches are not plausible** — they do not produce correct outputs even for the inputs in the validation suite.
2. **The overwhelming majority of the reported patches are not correct**, and are equivalent to a single modification that simply **deletes functionality**.
3. Observed negative effects include **introducing security vulnerabilities** and eliminating desirable standard functionality.
4. The authors then present **Kali**, a system that only deletes functionality, and show that it performs comparably to the repair systems on the same benchmarks.

Point 4 is the sharpest result for this thesis: a technique that does nothing but disable behaviour can look like a repair under a weak acceptance rule.

## Numbers worth citing

- Three systems analysed: GenProg, RSRepair, AE.
- "Majority" of reported patches not even plausible (the paper's own quantification is in the full text; the abstract states the direction, not a percentage).
- Kali: deletion-only and competitive.

## Why it matters for this thesis

This is the empirical ancestor of the `degenerat` verdict. The thesis defines *degenerat* as "the hole is closed but the legitimate action is blocked" — exactly the class Qi et al. show can dominate a benchmark when the gate only asks whether the exploit fails.

It also gives the thesis a precise argument for O2: an end-to-end flow is the minimum check that distinguishes "the attacker can no longer do X" from "nobody can do X". And it warns that evaluation-infrastructure bugs can make even a reasonable gate report the wrong thing — which is why the thesis hashes the test directory in O0 and validates O1's oracle on three trees before using it.

## Where to use it

- Related work, subsection on plausible vs. correct patches — with `smith2015` and `xiong2018`.
- Method, why the adjudication rubric has a separate `degenerat` class and why O2 exists.
- Threats to validity: a gate can be wrong because of infrastructure, not only because of the patch.

## Limits

The paper studies pre-LLM repair systems on classic benchmarks. It does not measure security patches specifically, and it does not compose multiple checks or report per-stage cost. It supports "the failure mode exists and is common", not "the thesis's gate will fail at rate X".
